Math Foundations for ML

Master the fundamental mathematics driving modern Machine Learning, from zero to advanced concepts.

0. Why Math?

The Language of Machine Learning

Mathematics is the underlying fabric of all machine learning algorithms. Understanding these concepts allows you to not just use algorithms as black boxes, but to build, debug, and optimize them.

1. Linear Algebra

The Mathematics of Data Structures

1A. Scalars, Vectors, Matrices

Data in ML is organized into arrays of different dimensions.

1B. Vector Operations (Dot Product)

The dot product is a fundamental operation that measures similarity between vectors (e.g., word embeddings).

a · b = |a||b|cos(θ) = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + ... + aₙbₙ

1C. Matrix Multiplication

Multiplying matrices involves taking the dot product of rows from the first matrix and columns from the second.

1D. Transpose and Inverse

Transpose (Aᵀ): Flips a matrix over its diagonal, switching rows and columns.

Inverse (A⁻¹): The matrix that, when multiplied by A, yields the Identity matrix (I).

A × A⁻¹ = I

1E. Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

When a linear transformation (matrix A) is applied to an eigenvector (v), the vector only scales by a factor (λ, eigenvalue), without changing direction.

Av = λv

Used heavily in PCA for dimensionality reduction.

2. Statistics & Probability

Understanding Data and Uncertainty

2A & 2B. Descriptive Stats, Variance, and Std Dev

Statistics help us summarize datasets. Variance and Standard Deviation (σ) measure how spread out the data is around the mean (μ).

σ = √[ Σ(xᵢ - μ)² / N ]

2C. Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

The bell curve is everywhere. The 68-95-99.7 rule dictates where data falls relative to standard deviations.

2D & 2E. Probability Basics & Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem updates our beliefs based on new evidence. It is the foundation of Naive Bayes classifiers.

P(A|B) = [ P(B|A) × P(A) ] / P(B)
3. Calculus for ML

Optimization and Rates of Change

3B. What is a Derivative?

The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change, or the slope of the tangent line at a specific point. It tells us how the output changes as we tweak the input.

f'(x) = d/dx (x²) = 2x

3C. Chain Rule (Backpropagation)

Used to compute derivatives of composed functions. In Neural Networks, it allows us to calculate gradients across multiple layers (Backpropagation).

dz/dx = (dz/dy) × (dy/dx)

3D. Partial Derivatives and Gradients

When a function has multiple variables (like weights in a neural network), we use partial derivatives. The gradient (∇f) is a vector of all partial derivatives. The negative gradient points towards the steepest descent (minimum loss).

4. Optimization Math

Finding the Minimum

4A. Convex vs Non-Convex

Convex: One global minimum (e.g., Linear Regression). Gradient descent always works.
Non-Convex: Multiple local minima (e.g., Deep Neural Nets). We can get stuck.

4C. Learning Rate (α) Effect

The step size we take in the direction of the negative gradient.

  • Too High: Oscillates, diverges.
  • Too Low: Takes forever to converge.
  • Optimal: Smooth convergence.

Interview Q&A

1. What is the dot product and why does ML use it?
The dot product is an algebraic operation that takes two equal-length sequences of numbers (vectors) and returns a single number. In ML, it's heavily used to calculate the similarity between two vectors (e.g., measuring how similar two word embeddings are) and is the core operation in neural network layers (weights dot inputs).
2. Why does gradient descent use the negative gradient?
The gradient of a function points in the direction of the steepest *ascent* (maximum increase). Since our goal in ML is to minimize the cost/loss function, we take steps in the opposite direction—the negative gradient—to reach the minimum.
3. What is the difference between variance and standard deviation?
Variance measures the average squared deviation of each number from the mean. Standard deviation is the square root of variance. We often prefer standard deviation because it is in the same units as the original data, making it easier to interpret.
4. Why is the normal distribution so important in ML?
Many natural phenomena follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution due to the Central Limit Theorem. Many ML algorithms (like Linear Regression and Gaussian Naive Bayes) assume the errors or data are normally distributed. It also simplifies calculations mathematically.
5. What is the chain rule and why is it essential for neural networks?
The chain rule is a formula to compute the derivative of a composite function. In neural networks, models are composed of nested functions (layers). The chain rule allows backpropagation to calculate how much each weight contributed to the final error by multiplying the local gradients backwards through the layers.
